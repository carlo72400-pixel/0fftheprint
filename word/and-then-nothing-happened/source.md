> Spotted: Trey Parker and Matt Stone crashing a writing class at NYU.

September 6, 2011. NYU Tisch, a course called Storytelling Strategies, filmed for an MTV show. The South Park guys walked in and taught it.

Parker gave the room one rule and said they'd maybe heard it before. Write your beats down. If AND THEN fits between two of them, you've got something boring. Between every beat goes BUT or THEREFORE.

He was right. It's old. Aristotle old. The Poetics already says it matters whether a thing happens because of the last thing, or just after it.

## Show them the bomb

Hitchcock to Truffaut, August 1962. Two people talk at a table and a bomb goes off. That's 15 seconds of surprise. Now show the audience the bomb first. And the clock. Same boring chat, 15 minutes of suspense.

But Hitchcock said on the tapes it wasn't new either. David Bordwell traced it back to Lessing's notes on drama from 1767. Buster Keaton put a bomb on a pool table in 1924.

## Every scene wants something

David Mamet once sent a memo to the writers of The Unit. In all caps. Ask every scene three things. Who wants what. What happens if they don't get it. Why now.

Aaron Sorkin says the same thing, shorter. "I worship at the altar of intention and obstacle." He told Interview in 2012 he can't write a scene until he knows what somebody wants and what's stopping them.

## But don't repeat what you heard

You heard Chekhov said a gun on the wall in act one has to go off in act three. What he actually wrote, in a letter in 1889, is that you don't put a loaded rifle on stage if nobody means to fire it. The pistol on the wall is how a friend, Ilia Gurlyand, remembered a talk with him. That got printed in 1904. None of the versions say act three.

You heard about Pixar's 22 rules. They're tweets. Emma Coats posted them in 2011, when she was a Pixar story artist. She never called them rules. Or Pixar's.
