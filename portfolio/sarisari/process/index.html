THE PROCESS

Everything that happens before the camera comes out of the bag.

You asked how I make this stuff. Here is the whole thing, in order, plus the films that taught me each part of it.

01 · The short version

I make content the way a film gets made.

Most social video is made by pointing a phone at something and hoping. Mine gets written first, drawn second, signed for third, and shot fourth. By the time I show up with a camera, everybody already knows what we are making, how long it runs, who is in it, and where it ends.

That is not me being fancy. It is the only way a shoot day finishes on time, and it is the only way you know what you are getting before you spend a dollar on it.

Nothing goes out that you have not watched first. Ever.
02 · The pipeline

Ten steps, every single time.

Same order for a sixty second commercial, an interview, or a birthday party. The scale changes, the order does not.

01
The talk What are you actually pushing this month, who do you want walking through the door, and what does the room look like on a normal Tuesday. Nothing gets written before that answer exists.
02
The treatment The whole piece written out in plain paragraphs. Opening image, what happens, how it feels, how it ends. Yours ran 244 words and it opens at the kitchen pass before the doors are unlocked and ends at the kamayan table. If it does not work on paper it will not work on a screen.
03
The script Two columns. Left side is everything you see, right side is everything you hear, lined up next to each other. Every shot, every spoken line, and the exact point where music starts and stops.
04
The shot list Every camera setup on one sheet: shot size, inside or outside, day or night, angle, how many seconds it runs, who is in it, which room. Grouped by room, so we shoot out one space completely and never drag the crew back and forth.
05
The boards The piece drawn frame by frame before shoot day. Yours is thirteen frames that add up to exactly sixty seconds. Anything that is not on the board does not get shot, and anything missing shows up now instead of at 9pm on the shoot day.
06
The paper Releases signed before we roll, not after. Full list in the next section.
07
The schedule Days picked around your service, not mine. Yours is three days, October 1, October 8, October 17, built off hours wherever possible so your guests never have to sign anything or be told to move.
08
Shoot day Kit matched to the room, not to what looks impressive. Two cameras, one on the face and one on the hands. The room light stays the room light. Nobody performs, people just do their job and talk while they do it.
09
Post I cut it, I color every shot by hand, I do the sound, and the music is written for the piece instead of borrowed. Borrowed music is how a good video gets muted or pulled six months later.
10
Your eyes, then delivery You watch it before the internet does. Changes come back to me, not to a comment section. Then it ships in the actual sizes you post in, plus the stills.
03 · The paperwork

Permissions are not an afterthought.

This is the part nobody talks about and it is the part that decides whether a video can stay online forever or has to come down. Every person in the frame and every room we stand in has a say in it, and the only version of a yes that counts is the one on paper.

  • Talent release, one per person on camera. Staff, owners, anyone recognizable.
  • Location release, one per room, plus a second copy signed at wrap when we walk out.
  • Copyright release from the business, because the name, the logo and the recipes are yours and not mine. Yours was signed August 19.
  • Music release, or no borrowed music at all, which is the route I take.
  • Crew paperwork for anybody I bring with me, so a second shooter is my responsibility and not yours.
  • Blank spares on shoot day, because somebody unplanned always walks in and ends up being the best thing in the video.

And when the answer is no, you get the reason.

A drone came up for one of the jobs. The answer was no. Any drone flight tied to a payment is a commercial flight, and it needs an FAA Part 107 certificate that attaches to the pilot, not to the rented aircraft.1 Hiring somebody without one puts the business on the hook too, not just the operator.2 So the answer was a gimbal walking shot instead: one take, in the front door, through the tables, landing on the person the night is about.

I would rather lose a shot than hand you a liability. When I do have the certificate, you will hear about it.

04 · The silent film

Three minutes, no dialogue, and that was the point.

This is the one that shows the directing. VOLUME is a short film I wrote, directed, shot and edited for my program. Nobody in it says a single word. It earned the Course Director's Award for directing in that class.

VOLUME (2026) · NARRATIVE SHORT · ★ COURSE DIRECTOR'S AWARD03:10

What making it silent actually taught me

Every shot has to say something alone.With no dialogue, the story lives in blocking, faces and cuts. If a shot is not doing a job it gets cut. That is the same test a video has to pass on a phone with the sound off, which is how most people are going to see yours.
Know the frame before you are in it.I was acting in most of it and operating the camera on all of it, so every shot I appear in is a locked off tripod I set and walked into. You learn very fast to design a shot instead of chasing one.
Scout with more than your eyes.I 3D scanned the library before the shoot and blocked the whole thing off site, so shoot day was execution instead of discovery.
Rehearse, then sign.Full cast rehearsal a week ahead. Every release, including the location, was signed that same day, four days before it was due. Nothing was left to shoot day.
The making of is content too.The shoot got covered as it happened and cut into a two part behind the scenes series. Same footage, second piece, no extra shoot day. That applies directly to a restaurant.
Short, and out.The note I got on it was that it gets in, gets out, and leaves people smiling. Three minutes. A commercial has sixty seconds and the same job.

05 · The interviews

People talk better while their hands are busy.

Every interview I shoot follows the same rules, and they came out of doing it wrong first.

Never a sit down against a wall.The subject works while they talk. The case, the wok, the register, the back door. People sound like themselves when their hands are doing something they know how to do.
You never hear me.My questions get cut out of the audio and become cards on screen that read ASKED OFF CAMERA. One voice the whole way through, so it plays like a piece and not like a Zoom call.
The music is written for it.Original score under the voice. Nothing gets claimed, muted, or pulled down later, which matters when the video is supposed to run for years.
Check the finished cut, not the notes.Every final piece gets transcribed back and checked line by line before it ships. I have caught my own voice in somebody else's mouth that way. Never again.
06 · The event work

The best idea on the job usually comes from the family.

The 80th birthday in August is the clearest example of how this goes when it is not a scripted piece.

Ask the host what they will want in a year.Not what looks good tonight. That is where the one big family photo and the ten separate family groups came from, and those are the frames that get printed.
The prayer was Adrian's idea.They pray before they eat, so we miked him and let the prayer carry the video instead of music. Best note in the entire job and it came from the client. My job was to hear it and build for it.
Plan the dark half before it arrives.Sun was down by 8 on a 7 to 9 party. The camera and the plan for the back half were picked in advance, not improvised when the room went orange.
Shoot past the order.The job called for thirty photos and a few videos. The cards came back with 201 frames and 35 clips. You get the set you asked for, and I keep the rest so the next thing you need already exists.
