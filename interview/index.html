01

Two Interviews

They are not the same job

One is three questions spread across a night while you are doing something else. The other is a sit-down on another day, in a room, with the machine running. Confusing the two is how somebody ends up holding a microphone at 1am being asked what their work means to them.

Three across a night

One question per act, hours apart, camera down. Each is answerable in a sentence while your hands are busy. It costs you a few minutes of the whole night and you are never sat down for it. The three are already written and already published on the format page.

A sit-down, another day

An hour or more, sober, doing your own work while you talk, never against a wall. That is a different commission with its own separate yes, and agreeing to one is not agreeing to the other. Six pieces cut from one of these are live on this site.

The same questions do not move between them, because the room is not the same room. A question with a run-up needs somewhere to land. In a doorway between two rooms with your friends within earshot, a long question gets answered for the room, and you can hear that in the take afterwards. So the night only gets the three that survive being asked standing up.

Three and not thirty, for a plainer reason. Thirty questions is a conversation, and a conversation means you spend your night talking to us instead of having the night. The night is the piece. We are there for three sentences of it.

You read the questions before we shoot. Not a summary of them. Them.

A question you have already seen is one you can refuse in advance, in writing, on a normal afternoon. That is worth more than any promise made in a room at 2am.

02

The Three

On the night  ·  one per act

These do not change from subject to subject. What changes is the noun inside them, because each one is built off something you already volunteered. The second only gets asked at all if it is true of you.

What do you have to get right to sound like them?
Act 1  ·  after the deciding is finished
Not in the first twenty minutes. That is not about catching you off guard, it is that a stranger with a camera is still a stranger for the first twenty minutes, and everything anybody says in that window is management rather than an answer. If it dies: give me one you got wrong.
You don't post. What does that get you?
Act 2  ·  outside, between two rooms
Outside because it is the only place quiet enough to record and you are standing still. Your friends in earshot is the point and not a compromise. Nobody gets separated out to be interviewed. If it dies: when's the last time you almost did?
What's something you wrote that you kept?
Act 3  ·  last, and easiest to refuse
It goes last because a thing you made is a bigger ask than a thing you think, so it comes after everything else and it is the one that costs least to say no to. It gets skipped entirely if the answer to you good with this one? is anything short of a clear yes. A two line piece is a normal outcome. If it dies: what would you never let somebody else write for you?

Every question and every backup is written out as a pair on paper first, the leading version next to the flat one, so a leading question gets caught on paper instead of in the room.

03

Not Asked

The shorter list, and the more useful one

If one of these gets asked anyway, it gets named out loud as a bad question and asked again straight, and the bad version stays in the cut.

  • How did that make you feel?
    It asks for a feeling. A feeling is a summary, and a summary is unusable. The version that works is what did that look like.
  • Anything about how you look
    The picture already did that. A question built on appearance turns you into the thing in the frame, and there is no answer to it that is not a defence.
  • Was that the hardest thing you've been through?
    It asks you to perform a wound on cue, and it is a yes or no wearing a coat. Nobody gets asked to be vulnerable on a schedule.
  • Is it more about this, or more about that?
    A menu. You take one of the two to be helpful, and the true answer was the third thing neither of us said.
  • Would you say that you're...?
    Our sentence in your mouth. If one comes out like this it gets named as leading, out loud, and asked again flat.
  • Who inspires you?
    The basic version, and it gets a basic answer every time. Whose stuff do you steal from gets a name and a story.
  • How long has it been?
    A scoreboard. It turns a person into a number and the number is the least interesting thing in the room.
  • Any word about your life you have not used first
    Yours get used. If you have not said it, it does not get said, and it does not go in a caption either.
  • What do you charge?
    You are not a client here and nothing gets pitched at you. How you started charging is a story. What you charge is your business.
  • So that's basically...?
    Tour guide. It hands you a definition and asks you to agree with it. Who put you on to that is a real question.
  • Anything that could cost you
    No addresses, no claiming, nothing in the present tense that somebody else could use against you later.
  • Anything asked for the first time after the first drink
    If it would not have been asked at eight it does not get asked at two. You can take things away all night. Adding waits for the morning.
  • Anything you already told us off camera
    Re-asking gets like I told you and that version is flatter every time. It gets written down and asked on a different day, or not at all.
  • Two questions in one breath
    You answer the easy one and the other is gone, and it is gone without either of us noticing.
04

The Bank

Sit-down format  ·  a separate yes

This belongs to the sit-down, which is its own commission. Agreeing to a night is not agreeing to this, and you are never shown this as a condition of that. It is here because the whole point of the page is that nothing is held back, including the questions for a thing you have not been offered.

A bank is not a script. It is what gets fallen back on when a good tangent dies, and most of it never gets asked. The order matters more than the count, and the order is the only part that is fixed. The questions get built off what you already said out loud, in person. The last time a set was built off somebody's public feed instead, it got thrown out and rebuilt the night before. That is the rule now. Nothing gets asked because it was found on your page.

The bank is nouns. Swap them and it runs for a tattooer, a DJ, a promoter, a cook. One becomes a line, a set, a night, a plate. Nothing else moves.

01The door
Open here. Everyone who makes anything has a name attached to it, almost nobody has been asked about it properly, and the answer is a story instead of an opinion.
  • where'd the name come from?
  • how old were you the first time you made one and it wasn't for school?
  • who took you?
  • is any of the early stuff still out there?
  • what's the difference between doing a lot of it and getting good at it?
02The hands
Asked while the work is happening. Hands busy, eyes down. Craft lives here and nothing heavy does.
  • talk me through what you're doing right now like i can't see it.
  • what part of this can you do without thinking?
  • what's the one that comes through the door so often you could do it asleep?
  • what's the difference between one that looks good and one that holds up?
  • how do you know when it's done?
03Who you took from
Never who inspires you. A basic question gets a basic answer. These get names and stories instead.
  • whose stuff do you steal from?
  • who taught you?
  • who was the first person who told you you were any good?
  • before the phone, what were you looking at?
  • where do you look that isn't this?
  • who around here is doing it right that nobody's saying anything about?
04What it cost
Not in the first forty five minutes, and never entered through the scoreboard version. These are easier to answer with something in your hands, and every one of them is easy to wave off.
  • who told you to get a real job?
  • what did you think you were going to lose if you kept going?
  • who'd you have to stop answering?
  • what was the first money you got for it, and what happened to it?
  • when's the closest you came to stopping?
  • what's the one you gave away that you wish you'd kept?
05The one nobody paid for
The part nobody has ever asked about. This is where a person gets to be an artist instead of a service, and it is usually the best five minutes on the tape.
  • what do you make that nobody asked for?
  • does anybody see it?
  • would you sell it? has anybody asked?
  • if they paid the same, which one do you do?
  • how does that one sit next to the work that pays?
06The closer
One question, held. It only works after the first five groups have put both halves of it on the table, and it never gets spent early to fill a silence.
  • one of these pays you and one of them doesn't. how did that happen?
SWAPWhen the craft is writing
Six drop-ins that replace their equivalents in groups two and five. Every craft gets a set like this built for it the day before, off what the person actually said.
  • what's the last thing you wrote that you'd read out loud?
  • where does it get written?
  • how do you know a line's finished?
  • what do you cut first when it's too long?
  • who do you hear when you read it back?
  • what's in there that nobody's read?
05

How We Ask

Camera down  ·  one question at a time
The follow-ups Seven rules, and you can hold us to every one
Your word, handed back
A follow-up takes a noun you already said and gives it back to you as a question. We do not swap our word in for yours. The moment the vocabulary changes you stop answering your own sentence and start answering our version of it, and the version that gets published is the one with our word sitting in the middle of it.
The example, never the meaning
We do not ask what a thing meant to you. We ask for the one time it happened. What did that look like. Which night. Who else was in the room. A meaning question gets a summary you have given before. It is the safest answer available to you and it is the one nobody remembers.
In, never out
Every follow-up goes smaller than the one before it. Never wider, and never up to what it says about anybody other than you. Reporters call it climbing down the ladder of abstraction, which makes it a working technique rather than a preference. The bottom of the ladder is where the thing you actually saw is.
No menus
Never two options. Never was it this or was it that. Two options is us writing your answer and letting you pick one. You take the easier one, everybody does, and then we have quoted ourselves and put your name on it.
Your sentence is yours to finish
If you stall, we wait. We do not supply the end of it. There is exactly one permitted interruption and it is wait, back up, do you mean, which hands the sentence back instead of taking it. The only reason to finish somebody's sentence is to get to the next question faster, and there is no next question worth that.
Nothing on the microphone
No yeah, no damn, no laugh. You get a nod and that is all you get. This is technical before it is manners. Our sound lands on top of your word and takes the whole sentence with it. It has happened here, in finished files, and they had to be pulled apart to fix it.
A leading question gets named
If a question comes out leading, we say out loud that it came out leading, and then ask it again flat. The alternative is pretending it was fine and keeping the answer, which leaves you quoted on a question you were steered into.
The pause
one question, then nothing.
three counts before anybody speaks.
the backup only comes out after the pause has died, never on top of you.

Three counts is much longer than it sounds when you are the one holding the recorder, which is why it is written down rather than left to instinct. The second half of an answer is the good half and it only arrives if nobody fills the gap.

06

Your Answer

Recorded, read back, cleared

Three separate events, and only the last one publishes anything. Being recorded is not being quoted, and being quoted back to you in the room is not clearance either.

The recording What is running, and how you stop it

A small transmitter, no cable, not plugged into the camera. It writes to its own memory, which means nothing is lost if our gear fails and a loud room cannot overload it. That is the good half.

The blunt half. Camera down does not mean nothing is running. It keeps recording after the questions are finished, it does not know the difference between an answer and you talking to your friend, and it will not stop because we walked away. So you pick how it works before it goes anywhere near you.

You arm it, you disarm it

The default. It is in your hand, you are shown the switch, and off means off. Hand it back whenever you want.

Only for the three

It goes on for the question and comes straight off. Most restrictive, and it costs the piece the least of any of the three.

You keep the file

You hold the recording and decide what we ever hear. Slowest option, and it has never once been a problem.

What switching off does not do

The camera has its own microphone for room sound. Turning yours off stops the thing listening only to you. It does not make the room silent, and we are not going to pretend it does.

The read-back Once, flat, exactly as typed
you said:
[ your line, read back exactly as it was typed ]
that right?

Not does that sound good and not can we use that. Those are different questions and one of them belongs much later.

This is the capture, not the clearance. It settles only whether the written sentence is the sentence you said. It settles nothing about whether it runs.

You never hear us. Questions are cut out rather than turned down, and they sit on the picture as type reading asked off camera. Every finished file gets transcribed back before it goes anywhere and has to come out as one speaker. That check has caught us on three separate occasions.

The clearance The parts that are only true here

The standing terms are on the format page and they are not restated here. These are the ones that exist because this is an interview.

  • You see the edit, not the rushes. The cut as it would run, before it runs anywhere.
  • You see the picture before you write the line. Not after, and not from a description of it.
  • Nothing gets edited for style. Grammar, slang, swearing, all of it stays. A mis-hear in the transcript gets fixed and that is the only correction we make.
  • A line about a named third party can be declined whole. You are told which line and why. Declining a line is not editing it, and it is protection for you before it is caution for us. A line that gets somebody sued gets you sued too.
  • You correct it rather than approve it. Marking a line wrong is expected, not a complaint.
  • Your name is its own decision. First name, a handle, a name you pick, or nothing at all. The piece works without it.
  • No publish date exists until after the yes. There is no calendar with your name on it and nothing is waiting on you.
  • Nothing is held over you. Saying no to this costs you nothing here, now or later.
Where this actually lives, in plain words. This site is built in the open. The code and every image in it are public, and anybody can copy the whole thing. A line you pull comes off the page straight away and stays in the code history until that history is rewritten. A screenshot or a repost is past anybody's reach, ours included. That is why the second yes gets asked before publication rather than after, and it is why nobody here will promise you a delete button.

If you go quiet

A dead question stays dead for the night. It does not come back in a different shape an hour later, and it is never re-asked the same day, because the second telling is flatter and you would be spending your own best version on a retake.

And we do not go looking for it

We do not fish for it in the edit, we do not cut around the gap to imply an answer, and a two line piece runs as a two line piece.

A piece has already run here with its strongest answers cut out of it, because somebody in the room had not signed anything and never did. It ran short rather than running without her. That material still exists, it has still never been published, and it is the same trade every time.

The Shelf

8 works  ·  every link opened and checked

None of the rules on this page are ours. They were written down first by people who record other people for a living, and this is where they came from, so you can open them and check us against them. Everything here was fetched and read before it went on, and anything that came back dead or paywalled got cut. Two are marked load bearing, meaning they set a rule that is now in force. Two of them argue against the way this house works, and they stayed on anyway.

How it gets asked The question, the silence, the follow up
Introduction to oral history
Baylor University Institute for Oral History · 2016 · PDF manual
A free nineteen part manual for people who record other people, and it teaches not leading as matched pairs. Not: Wasn't Sandtown a poor, hard place to grow up in? But: Tell me how you felt about the place where you grew up. It says ask one question at a time. It says Use the silent question. Keep quiet and wait. It hands the read back its exact words, Let me say that back to you and see if I understand. You said . . . And its ethics page says do not subject narrators to biased assumptions, avoid bringing them undue notoriety, and give narrators the chance to review what they said.
What to takeWrite every question out twice before the night, the leading version and the flat one, and carry only the flat one into the room. Then stop talking and let the silence do the asking. And the reason nobody hums agreement while a narrator is mid answer is not manners, it is that the sound lands on top of the word and takes the line with it.

Studs Terkel, in conversation
Studs Terkel with Sydney Lewis · Transom · 2001
Terkel on his own method after forty five years of it. Listen. The first word is listen. You listen. You hear that person. He is openly bad with the machine, I goof up on tape recorders, I press the wrong button, and being visibly not a professional operator is the part that loosens a room. On what to chase: Why's this guy laughing, you see? Why'd that woman suddenly stop . . . and go on to something else? Well, let it ride, but then come back to it later. And on limits, flatly: Sometimes there are personal things you don't pry into.
What to takeKeep the recorder visible and do not run it slick, because slick is what makes a room perform. When somebody stops mid sentence or laughs in the wrong place, write the time down and leave it alone, then come back to it two hours later. That is the one thing a night this long is actually good for. And name what you will not pry into out loud, instead of quietly steering around it.

Filling in scenic details
Kim Cross · Nieman Storyboard · 25 July 2024
A working narrative reporter's actual follow up set for turning an abstraction back into something that happened. What did that look like. How so. What do you mean. She calls it working down the Ladder of Abstraction, asking which hand, which finger, what colour, what shape, on the rule that the more concrete the detail the crisper the image. She allows exactly one interruption: Wait. Let's back up. Do you mean . . . ?
What to takeMake what did that look like the standing second question after all three. Work down the ladder in the room until an idea turns back into a thing with a colour and a time on it. And keep wait, let's back up, do you mean as the only permitted interruption, because it stops short of finishing the sentence and hands it straight back.

What you are owed Consent, review, and the second yes
Interviewing people who have never been interviewed
Kelly McBride · Poynter · 22 August 2023
NPR reporters on working with sources who have no media training. Joe Shapiro makes sure they know what NPR even is, and tells them that as well as the radio there will be a text version on the internet that will live forever. Hanisha Harjani let a story go when a source asked to back out, because it's really important for vulnerable sources to feel like they have control in this relationship. Stacey Vanek Smith emails the source the details and characterisations before it airs. McBride's frame: with a private person, the power advantage and the accompanying responsibility shifts to the journalist.
What to takeSay all of it out loud at the start instead of leaving it on a page to be read. What this magazine is, where the piece goes, and that a web page does not evaporate. Then run the second yes as a correction pass rather than a verdict. Here is how you are described, fix what is wrong, instead of here is the edit, yes or no.

Statement on ethics
Oral History Association · the field's own standard
The profession holding itself to something. Consent means documenting the knowing agreement of the narrator to take part. Interviewers must communicate the narrator's right to refuse to answer questions. And the clearance rule almost word for word: that the narrator be given the opportunity to review the interview (recording and/or transcript) and approve what was said for public release. The best practices page alongside it adds the right to restrict access, or to choose a pseudonym.
What to takeThe pseudonym is standing here now and nobody has to ask for it. So is holding part of a tape back from public access. And take the word: narrator, not subject. A subject is what a camera has. A narrator is the person whose words those are, which on a piece whose spine is somebody's own writing is the accurate one.

Writers at Work, first series
Introduced by Malcolm Cowley · Viking · 1958
Cowley's introduction explains how the Paris Review interviews were actually made. There was no recording equipment, so the interviewers usually worked in pairs, like FBI agents, both writing at speed and matching the two versions afterwards. Then: After two or three sessions the interviewers typed up their material; then it was cut to length, arranged in logical order, and sent to the author for his approval. E. M. Forster was the first subject and turned the whole series into a conversation about craft.
What to takeNobody gets to call the second yes amateur. The most respected interview series in the language has sent the material back for approval since 1958 and prints that fact in the front of the book. Take the shape as well. Two or three sittings, then cut to length and put in order, which is three acts across one night rather than one long unbroken tape.

The case against us Kept on the shelf on purpose
Should sources look over stories first
Thomas Kent · Poynter · 12 May 2020
The mainstream newsroom position against most of this shelf. Kent: Our right to quote material from sources as we heard it, in the fashion we want, is a precious one, and reporters should never agree in advance to show an interviewee their quotes or the finished story, because it manufactures the appearance of source veto power. He draws one line though. Calling a source to clarify a point, or reading quotes back to check the facts, is accuracy work and is fine.
⚠ This is a real argument and this house breaks it on purpose. Read it before taking our word for anything else on this page.
What to takeSay the break out loud rather than hoping nobody notices it. The case against quote approval exists to stop powerful people editing accountability journalism, and none of that describes a private person being asked about their own night. Kent's own line also puts the read back on his side of it. Reading a quote back is the accuracy check every newsroom already allows. The second yes is the extra thing on top.

The Journalist and the Murderer
Janet Malcolm · The New Yorker, March 1989 · Knopf, 1990
It opens: Every journalist who is not too stupid or too full of himself to notice what is going on knows that what he does is morally indefensible. Her argument is that the journalist wins access through friendship and intimacy and then presents the person in a way that person never anticipated or approved. The Internet Archive files it as a study of the psychopathology of journalism, which is roughly how it reads.
⚠ The mechanism she names is exactly this format's risk. Access through intimacy at night, and then a piece the person never saw coming.
What to takeKeep her on the shelf instead of off it. The two rules that answer her are already written down. The narrator sees the finished piece before anybody else does, and the yes that publishes it is a sober one on a different day. Name the worst case first, in the house's own words, before somebody else names it for you.
