All Night

One person. One night. Three acts. You set the rule, and we find out together whether it held.

You pick what the night is about  ·  You see it before anyone
Anything you want out is out, no reason needed
01

The Idea

Most photographs of a night out are a record of what happened. There is nothing to measure them against, so they are just output. Thirty thousand people shoot the same street on the same Friday and none of it means anything, because nobody wrote down first what the night was supposed to be.

You say what the night is at eight. At three we find out if it held.

That is the whole piece. A control at the front, in your words, on the record, and an answer at the back, in your words. It is not a story about how you looked at the beginning and how you looked at the end. Everybody already knows what happens to a face by 3am, and that is a photograph anyone can take.

So the thing that repeats across the three acts is not your face. It is one line from you per act, written or said in the moment, and they run on the page in the biggest type on it. The pictures carry the night. The lines carry the argument.

02

The Rule

You pick it, not us

Before anything is shot, you name one rule for the night. Not a theme, not a look, not an outfit. A rule, because a rule is something you can actually break.

The only brief you get
pick one rule for tonight.
something you could actually break, so at the end we know if it held.

A look can be photographed off your body, which means the camera decides whether it survived and you had nothing to do with it. A rule can only be kept or broken by you. That is the difference, and it is why the last act is yours to answer instead of ours to shoot.

What we do with it

It becomes the title of the piece. It sets the colour of the type on the page. It decides which rooms we bother going into. Everything the house brings is built around the thing you named, which means it changes completely from one subject to the next. That is the point of the format.

If you don't have one

Fine. We shoot the night and you name it afterwards, and the piece runs without a rule. It is a weaker piece and we will say so, but a rule you invented to be polite is worse than none, and nobody has to perform a concept they don't believe.

03

Three Acts

All frames below are ours

Three acts, because a night has three completely different lighting problems in it and pretending otherwise is how these pieces end up looking like everyone else's. Every photograph on this page is from the house archive. It is what the register already looks like, not a mood board of other people's work.

ACT 1Getting ReadyWarm, controlled, one room
What the act is for
The decision, on the record, before the first drink. Not who you are. What you decided.
The turn
The moment choosing becomes committing. Three things on the bed, you take the second one, the other two go back.
It only counts if
You say the rule out loud, once, and it gets written down with a time on it. If the act does not produce that line, the act did not happen no matter how many frames came out of it.
What we never shoot
Anything wide enough to identify your building, your street or your door.
ACT 2Going OutGel, crowd, no control at all
What the act is for
The pressure test. What the rule costs you to keep once the night is actually running.
The turn
The first time the night pushes back, and you either adjust or you hold.
It only counts if
There is at least one frame where the rule is visibly under strain and you are still running it. Twenty good group photographs are not this act.
The trap
This act hands back the most frames and the fewest that are about anything. It gets cut hardest.
ACT 33AMFluorescent, flat, nobody art directed it
What the act is for
The answer. You say whether the thing you named at eight is still true, and that is the end of the piece.
Why it is not sad
The cliche version of 3am is a tired face under bad light and a slow song. We do not grade this act to be pretty and we do not grade it to be bleak. It is the least treated thing in the piece on purpose, because the rule has to survive a room nobody styled.
It only counts if
Your answer is yours. If you are done, the act runs with no line and the piece ends on two. That is a normal outcome, not a failure.
04

The Register

How it is shot

The look is hot and saturated, the grit is grain rather than photocopy, and the black graphic furniture goes on top of the photograph instead of into it. Messy on purpose. A little unclean beats polished, every time.

Wide, and close
Standing inside the thing rather than watching it from across the street. The one real lesson from the found-footage party film everybody cites: it was shot on a professional cinema camera chosen for how it survives sudden light changes, then made to look amateur on top. The roughness is engineered. It is not what happens when you stop trying.
Whoever holds the camera is in the story
The camera gets passed around. Frames shot by you or your friends are credited to you or your friends, by name, on the page. Not a gimmick. It is a field in the file, so a frame you shot is a frame you own and can pull on your word alone.
One colour owns one scene
Instead of correcting a room back to neutral, we let a single found colour take the whole frame and hold it for a long beat. Sciamma's Girlhood does this better than anything: four girls in one blue room, performing for each other and not for the camera, no escalation and no consequence waiting at the end.
Your eye line, never above or below
Nothing shot up at you and nothing shot down at you. It is in the paperwork, not just the plan.
A hard light on the lens axis
Direct flash, when it is used, is a declaration rather than a style. It separates one person from a crowd and it admits out loud that somebody was standing there with a camera. It is also the only light that works in all three acts, which is the practical half of the argument.
Your words are the biggest thing on the page
Three lines, one per act, set larger than anything else. The photographs are the evidence. The lines are the piece.

The Shelf

36 works  ·  every link opened and checked

Real outside work, named so it can be opened rather than reproduced here. Everything on this shelf was fetched and read before it went on, and anything that came back dead, or paywalled, or landing on the wrong page, got cut. Nine are marked load bearing, meaning they change how the night is actually shot. The rest are corrections, warnings, and things to steal one move from.

The spine Her words are the picture, and somebody already solved this
Rich and Poor
Jim Goldberg · 1977-85 · Magnum
Goldberg photographed people in their own homes, then took the print back to them and had them write on it, by hand. The captions argue with the photographs instead of describing them. I am doomed to be in this place, I have no future. Poverty sucks but it brings us closer together.
What to takeThis is the mechanism, and it is a procedure rather than a look. One anchor frame per act, printed or on a screen, handed to her, and she writes the line ON it in her own hand. Then shoot the handwriting and set that as the display type instead of a font. Two rules copied straight off him: she writes only after seeing the picture, and not one word gets edited.

Signs that say what you want them to say
Gillian Wearing · 1992-93 · Tate
Strangers in Regent's Park holding a card with their own sentence on it. One 35mm body, one or two frames each, no posing. About 80 percent said yes.
What to takeThe authorship split: he owns the frame, she owns the words, and the words sit physically inside the photograph. And her failure note is the whole warning. She tried it first on people she knew and it did not work, because they wrote what they thought she wanted to hear. Do not tell her what the piece is about before she writes the line.

As it may be
Bieke Depoorter · 2011-17 · Magnum
She went back years later with a draft of the book and let people write across their own photographs in pen and pencil. The refusals stayed in. I don't want this book to be published. You have lived through one snapshot, an hour or two, maybe a night. Just a picture.
What to takeRun the selects past her before publication, hand her a pen, and print what she says including where she disagrees with you. That last line is the exact charge a one-night cover story has to answer, so answer it inside the piece.

Jörg Colberg · Hyperallergic, 2013
Written from inside the period. His argument is that the dashboard fragments intentional sequences, reblogging pulls single frames out of their relationships, and attribution is too thin to trace anyone back to their real body of work.
What to takeTake his workaround as a layout rule. Act 1 One room, before
Girls' Night
Eimear Lynch, written by Tiarna Meehan · Dazed, 2024
Irish teenagers photographed getting ready as they normally would. Sharing clothes, doing each other's faces. Shot on one camera and one handheld flash specifically to stay inconspicuous, and laid out like a diary with handwritten entries running through it.
What to takeThe kit is the point. One small body and nothing else is what keeps you a fly on the wall instead of a production. And put the mirror in the frame: at f/1.8 you get her, her reflection and the room in one exposure. Cite the ritual and the kit here, never the demographic, because the subjects are minors.

Too Close
eugenia bakanova · Are.na channel
A working photographer's actual reference file for a direct flash experiment, with her own notes attached to each name. On Corinne Day: wait for the pose to collapse. On Mark Cohen: get too close, cut away the context. And the line that is the whole channel: I don't need to create a scene. I need to notice when one already exists.
What to takeSteal her seven step sequence as the literal shot order for act 1: ordinary life, intimacy, too close, direct flash, imperfect exposure, fragment, ambiguity. Start on the room and the objects before you frame her at all. The fragment is the operative one for a person with no crew, and it is also the structural escape from shooting her as something to look at.

Girlhood, the Diamonds scene
Céline Sciamma, reviewed by Catherine Knight · 4:3
Four girls in a blue lit hotel room lip syncing to each other, dressed up for nobody but themselves. The whole song plays out rather than getting cut into a montage, which is why it reads as sustained instead of as a flicker of silliness.
What to takeLet one track run to its end in one unbroken take while they perform to each other and not to the lens. Set the camera down at chest height, lock exposure and white balance manually before you roll so it cannot hunt, shoot into one colour the room already has. Run the take long enough that she stops noticing it. Break before forty minutes or the Luna screen dies and takes the recording with it.

Adrienne Salinger · 1995 · AnOther

Brooklyn Gang
Bruce Davidson · 1959 · Magnum
A summer with a Brooklyn teenage gang. Davidson: I found myself involved with a group of unpredictable youths who were mostly indifferent to me.

Act 2 The room, the street, the crowd
The Buzz Club
Rineke Dijkstra · 1996-97 · MACBA
She built an improvised studio inside two working nightclubs, a plain backdrop and one light, and taped clubbers standing in front of it one at a time while the room's music kept going. Some look straight down the lens. Some carry on dancing alone.
What to takeThe removal, and it is the single most useful idea for act 2. Find one blank wall next to the room, a corridor, a stairwell, the back of the bar, and shoot everything there instead of on the floor, one person at a time, with the music still on their faces. It also solves the light: a plain wall a foot behind her means one door light does the whole exposure.

Provisional Notes for a Disappeared City
Reynaldo Rivera, written by Jesse Dorris · Aperture
Trans women, drag performers and scenesters in LA bars in the late eighties and nineties. Subjects straight on and centre framed, the photographer visible in mirror reflections inside the shot. The book ends with a conversation between Rivera and one of his subjects.
What to takeShoot from inside the frame rather than across it. Let the mirror carry the room, which is the only way a small sensor gets depth out of a tight space, and it puts you in the picture as a participant. Centre her, frontal, the framing you use for someone presenting something she made. Let the shadow stay black.

The Gleaners and I
Agnès Varda with Chris Darke · Sight and Sound, 2001
Varda on shooting alone with a small camera, and on the shot of her own ageing hand: One hand filmed and the other hand was the subject. Her working definition: a film is a slice of life, it's not a production.
What to takePut the operator inside the piece on purpose. Keep rolling while you talk so your questions are on the track and she is answering a person. Film your own hand, your reflection, and the physical hand off when the camera goes to a friend. Those frames are the shot-by credit. An audible operator forecloses the anonymous observer register, because there is no anonymous observer left in it.

Unfinished Sympathy
Baillie Walsh · 1991 · Promonews
One rule executed once: a single unbroken take travelling ahead of Shara Nelson down one stretch of LA street. Three complete takes existed and the director hated all of them, and still resents that she outpaces the camera and drifts out of frame.
What to takeOne rule, one take, one street. Walk backwards ahead of her for a whole track at chest height and do not stop for anything entering frame. Lock exposure first or a backward walk under streetlight pumps the whole way. Then take his own complaint as permission: the take where she outpaces you is the keeper.

White Night
Feng Li · from 2005 · It's Nice That
Chengdu, shot for twenty years by a civil servant in his spare time with no planning. Flash punches each subject out of a dark surround so it reads as one continuous night whatever hour it was. Absurd and theatrical rather than sad.
What to takeSubtraction, not fill. Find the one place a hard source already exists, a doorway light, a phone torch a friend is holding, the beam over the DJ, stand her in it and expose for that alone, letting the room fall to black. Act 3 After
A1: The Great North Road
Paul Graham · 1981-83 · Huxley-Parlour
Roadside cafes and service stations in colour when British documentary was still black and white. The works list is the reference: Ashtray on Table, Morley's Cafe. Bible, Drivers' Rest Room, Blyth Services. Little Chef in Rain, St. Neots.
What to takeTreat the 3am counter as a warm room. Overhead fluorescent off formica is orange and yellow green once you balance to it, so never let that grade go blue. Sleepers / After Party
Dhruv Malhotra, essay by David Campany
Long exposures of people asleep outdoors, and then the venues photographed after the celebration ended. Campany's description of the second: dining chairs swagged in fabric and ribbon, empty platforms for speeches, floors strewn with trash, all bathed in an acrid, eye-watering, artificial light.
What to takeThe room the night happened in, shot after it emptied, trash still down. Brace on a table or a curb, drop to base ISO, run one to eight seconds. Do not try for minutes, the LED sources will clip. She sits still inside it or walks through and smears. If a venue lets you back in after close, that is one frame and it ends the act.

The digicam is back
Alina Selyukh · NPR, 2026
Reported with real figures instead of trend assertion. The look named precisely: grainy, overexposed in the centre, blinding flash, often date stamped. One source on why she carries one: you put it in your bag, you don't think about it, and then at the end of the night, you go through all the photos and kind of relive that moment.

Bloody Nose, Empty Pockets
Bill and Turner Ross, reviewed by Sophie Brown · Sight and Sound
One continuous shift in a dive bar on its closing night. The colour is named twice in the review: cosy, woozy, rose dipped.

Three Years of Big Nights Out
Clive Martin · VICE, 2016
The writer's own post mortem on the series he built. Looking back, he says a lot of what was said about the people in the pictures was probably unwarranted, played into stereotypes, and was largely a bit mean.

Power, Consent, and Obligations
Jörg Colberg · Conscientious, 2020
His argument is that consent is the floor and not the ethics. Getting permission settles nothing and only moves the question to what obligations come with telling someone else's story. He works it through Migrant Mother, whose subject consented and spent the rest of her life resenting it.

Three Questions

One per act, camera down

Not an interview. Three questions across eight hours, each one answerable in a sentence while you are doing something else. They get typed out, read back to you once, and you can change them or kill them.

What do you have to get right to sound like them?
Act 1  ·  after the outfit is decided, before anyone arrives
Never in the first twenty minutes, when a stranger with a camera is still a stranger with a camera and everything said is management. If it dies: give me one you got wrong.
You don't post. What does that get you?
Act 2  ·  outside, between two rooms, friends within earshot
Outside because it is the only place quiet enough to record, standing still, and nothing is being asked of anyone alone. If it dies: when's the last time you almost did?
What's something you wrote that you kept?
Act 3  ·  the quietest place left, after the food lands
This one gets skipped entirely if the answer to "you good with this one?" is anything less than a clear yes. A two line piece is fine. If it dies: what would you never let somebody else write for you?

Follow-ups repeat your own word back as a question and ask for the example, never the meaning. No menus, no finishing your sentence, no reacting on the microphone. If a question comes out leading it gets named as leading and asked again straight.

07

The Paper

Read before anything is shot

This part is not fine print and it is not a formality. It is most of why the format works, and all of it is agreed before the first frame.

  • You see it before anybody else does. Every frame, graded, and the cut. Before it is anywhere.
  • Anything you want out is out. No reason required, no negotiation, no asking twice.
  • Yes twice. Once before, once after you have seen the edit, on a different day and sober. It publishes when you say yes, and if that never comes it never runs.
  • After the first drink you can take things away, you cannot add them. If you want to widen it, that waits for the morning.
  • Your friends get asked by you, days ahead, in writing. A stranger asking in front of you is the least free room there is. Anyone who has not answered is out of every frame.
  • Two of three, never all three. The time, the room and a face you can recognise. The piece publishes at most two. The venue stays out of the page.
  • The microphone is yours to switch off. It records to itself, so it does not stop when the camera does. You are told exactly how it comes off, and it comes off whenever you want.
  • Nothing shot up at you or down at you. Your eye line, all night.
  • What we cannot promise. The site is built in the open, so a file that gets pulled stays in the code history until that history is rewritten. It comes off the page immediately. It does not evaporate. Anybody who tells you otherwise is guessing.
